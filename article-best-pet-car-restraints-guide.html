1. Why Crash Testing Matters

Despite the pet industry's staggering financial worth, there is surprisingly little regulation governing the safety of pet travel products[reference:19]. The Center for Pet Safety (CPS) is the leading independent authority on pet product crash testing[reference:20].

CPS looks at "excursion measurements" – how far the pet moves in a crash[reference:21]. A harness labeled "crash tested" should specify which protocol it passed (simulated dog weight, sled velocity, attachment method)[reference:22]. In 2026, ASTM International approved a new standard for dog harnesses used in motor vehicles[reference:23].

4. What to Look For

  • Independent crash testing – look for "CPS certified" or "crash-tested" with protocol details
  • Heavy-duty metal clips – not plastic, which can break under force
  • No elastic bungee sections – allow too much forward movement ("excursion")
  • Harness design that avoids the neck – neck-only attachments cause spinal injuries
  • ASTM compliance – look for products meeting the new ASTM F15 standard[reference:33]

7. What to Avoid

  • Attaching to a collar instead of a harness – can break the neck in an accident
  • Elastic tethers – allow too much forward movement
  • Non-crash-tested products – provide little to no protection
  • Products without independent testing – marketing claims are not safety guarantees
