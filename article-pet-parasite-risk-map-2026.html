📊 Key Finding: CAPC forecasts, built on more than a decade of surveillance data, have historically exceeded 94% accuracy. For 2026, the forecast projects continued geographic expansion of four key vector-borne diseases: Lyme disease, heartworm, ehrlichiosis, and anaplasmosis.[reference:2]

1. The Changing Map of Parasite Risk

The parasite risk map is shifting. Comparing 2022 and 2025 American Heartworm Society incidence maps reveals a clear trend: high-incidence areas are pushing north along the Mississippi River corridor and the Atlantic coast. New pockets of elevated risk are appearing in places like the Mountain West, Northern California, and the Northern Plains.[reference:3]

What's driving this shift? A convergence of factors:

  • Warmer temperatures – allowing ticks and mosquitoes to survive and thrive in areas where they couldn't before[reference:4]
  • Animal movement – when animals (and people) move around, they bring parasites with them[reference:5]
  • Geographic spread – steady movement of Lyme disease from the Northeast westward, rapid increase of anaplasmosis along the East Coast, and tick species moving into new regions[reference:6]

3. What This Means for Your Pet

If you live in or travel to areas where these diseases are emerging, your pet may be at risk even if you've never seen a tick or mosquito in your yard. Key considerations:

  • Year-round prevention: Many regions now require year-round parasite prevention, not just seasonal
  • Travel risks: If you travel with your pet, you may bring parasites back to your home region
  • Testing: Annual heartworm and tick-borne disease testing is more important than ever

4. Updated Prevention Strategies for 2026

With the changing risk map, prevention strategies need to evolve. The Companion Animal Parasite Council recommends:

  • Year-round heartworm prevention – in all regions, not just warm climates
  • Broad-spectrum tick prevention – covering multiple tick species
  • Annual testing – for heartworm and tick-borne diseases, even in low-risk areas
  • Environmental management – yard treatments and tick checks after outdoor activities

6. What to Avoid

  • Assuming your area is still low-risk – the map is changing
  • Seasonal prevention only – many regions now require year-round protection
  • Not testing annually – early detection improves treatment outcomes
  • Ignoring travel risks – pets can bring parasites home from trips

7. Your Action Plan
