📊 Prevalence: Feline hyperthyroidism is one of the most common endocrine disorders in cats, affecting up to 10% of cats over 10 years of age. It is rare in cats under 8 years old. The condition is characterized by excessive production of thyroid hormones, leading to increased metabolism and a range of clinical signs.

1. What Is Feline Hyperthyroidism?

Hyperthyroidism occurs when the thyroid glands produce excessive amounts of thyroid hormones (T4 and T3). This speeds up the body's metabolism, causing weight loss, increased appetite, and a host of other symptoms. The condition is almost always caused by benign functional adenomas (tumors) of the thyroid gland.

2. Early Warning Signs

  • Weight loss — despite a ravenous appetite (most common sign)
  • Increased appetite (polyphagia) — eating more than usual
  • Increased thirst and urination (polydipsia/polyuria)
  • Hyperactivity or restlessness
  • Vomiting or diarrhea
  • Poor coat quality — unkempt, matted, or greasy coat
  • Behavioral changes — irritability, aggression, or anxiety
⚠️ If your senior cat shows these signs, schedule a veterinary exam immediately. Early detection improves treatment outcomes.

3. Diagnosis

  • Total T4 (thyroxine) level: The initial screening test. Elevated levels confirm hyperthyroidism.
  • Free T4 (fT4): More sensitive test, useful if total T4 is borderline.
  • Thyroid scintigraphy (radioactive iodine scan): Detects abnormal thyroid tissue and differentiates between benign and malignant growths.
  • Bloodwork and urinalysis: To assess kidney function (important because hyperthyroidism can mask kidney disease).

5. Managing Chronic Kidney Disease with Hyperthyroidism

Many hyperthyroid cats also have underlying kidney disease. Treating hyperthyroidism can unmask kidney issues, requiring careful monitoring. Your vet may recommend a treatment plan that balances thyroid and kidney management, often starting with a lower dose of methimazole and gradually adjusting.

7. What to Avoid

  • Ignoring early signs — untreated hyperthyroidism can cause heart disease and kidney damage
  • Self-medicating — always use veterinary-prescribed medications
  • Skipping follow-up bloodwork — regular monitoring is essential
  • Stopping medication abruptly — can cause thyroid storm

8. Your Action Plan

  1. If your cat shows signs of hyperthyroidism, schedule a vet exam
  2. Discuss treatment options with your vet (methimazole, I-131, surgery, diet)
  3. Start treatment and monitor for improvement
  4. Schedule regular T4 rechecks (every 3-6 months initially)
  5. Monitor kidney values and adjust treatment as needed

Final thought: Feline hyperthyroidism is a common, treatable condition. With proper diagnosis and treatment — whether medication, radioactive iodine, or dietary management — most cats can live long, healthy lives. The cost of treatment ($50-$200/month for methimazole, $1,500-$3,000 for I-131) is a worthwhile investment in your cat's quality of life.
