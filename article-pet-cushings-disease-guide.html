Cushing's Disease in Dogs: Diagnosis & Long-Term Management (2026)

  • Early signs: increased thirst, increased urination, pot-bellied appearance

When 9-year-old Miniature Poodle, Bella, started drinking excessive water, having accidents in the house, and developing a pot-bellied appearance, her owner thought it was just old age. A routine blood panel revealed elevated liver enzymes, and further testing confirmed Cushing's disease – a condition where the adrenal glands produce too much cortisol. With daily medication and regular monitoring, Bella's symptoms improved significantly. "I had no idea Cushing's was so common," her owner says. "Now I know what to look for."

📊 Prevalence: Cushing's disease (hyperadrenocorticism) is one of the most common endocrine disorders in dogs, affecting primarily middle-aged to older dogs (average age 10-12 years). Certain breeds are predisposed, including Poodles, Dachshunds, Boston Terriers, and Yorkshire Terriers.

1. What Is Cushing's Disease?

Cushing's disease occurs when the adrenal glands produce excessive cortisol – a hormone that regulates metabolism, immune function, and stress response. It can be caused by:

  • Pituitary-dependent Cushing's (PDH): A benign tumor in the pituitary gland (accounts for 80-85% of cases)
  • Adrenal-dependent Cushing's: A tumor on one of the adrenal glands (accounts for 15-20% of cases)
  • Iatrogenic Cushing's: Caused by long-term use of corticosteroid medications

2. Signs of Cushing's Disease

  • Polydipsia (excessive thirst) – drinking from water bowls more frequently
  • Polyuria (excessive urination) – accidents in the house, needing to go out more often
  • Pot-bellied appearance – abdomen becomes distended and pendulous
  • Skin changes – thinning skin, hair loss (especially on the trunk), dark spots
  • Increased appetite (polyphagia) – constantly hungry
  • Lethargy and muscle weakness – reduced activity levels
  • Panting – excessive, even when not hot
⚠️ If your dog shows multiple signs, especially excessive thirst and a pot-bellied appearance, consult your vet for testing.

3. Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires specialized testing:

  • ACTH stimulation test: Measures cortisol response to ACTH injection. Elevated levels indicate Cushing's.
  • Low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (LDDST): Measures cortisol suppression after dexamethasone. Failure to suppress indicates Cushing's.
  • Abdominal ultrasound: Visualizes the adrenal glands and checks for tumors.
  • Bloodwork and urinalysis: Elevated liver enzymes (ALP), dilute urine, and cholesterol elevation are common.

4. Treatment Options

Trilostane is currently the first-line treatment for most dogs with Cushing's disease. It's effective, well-tolerated, and requires regular monitoring.

5. Monitoring and Long-Term Management

  • Regular ACTH stimulation tests: Monitor cortisol levels (every 1-3 months initially)
  • Monitor for side effects: Signs of hypoadrenocorticism (weakness, vomiting, diarrhea)
  • Dose adjustments: Based on test results and clinical signs
  • Diet: High-quality protein, low fat (to reduce liver strain)
  • Weight management: Prevent obesity, which worsens the condition

6. Real Owner Success Story

"My 10-year-old Dachshund, Oscar, was diagnosed with Cushing's after I noticed he was drinking water constantly and having accidents. He started trilostane twice daily, and within weeks his symptoms improved significantly. He's been on medication for 18 months and is doing well." – Jennifer, Texas

7. What to Avoid

  • Ignoring early signs – untreated Cushing's can cause serious complications
  • Missing dose adjustments – cortisol levels need regular monitoring
  • Giving high-fat foods – can worsen liver strain
  • Stopping medication abruptly – can be dangerous

8. Your Action Plan

  1. If you notice signs of Cushing's, schedule a vet visit
  2. Work with your vet to perform diagnostic testing (ACTH, LDDST)
  3. Start treatment (usually trilostane) and schedule monitoring tests
  4. Monitor your dog's symptoms and report changes to your vet
  5. Adjust medication as needed

Final thought: Cushing's disease is a manageable condition with the right treatment and monitoring. The cost of medication ($30-$80/month) and monitoring tests ($150-$300 per visit) is significant, but the improvement in your dog's quality of life is invaluable. Work closely with your vet to keep your dog comfortable and healthy.
