1. What Is FIP?

Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP) is a severe, progressive disease caused by a mutated form of the feline coronavirus. The virus infects macrophages (immune cells), leading to widespread inflammation and damage to blood vessels. There are two forms:

  • Wet (effusive) form: Accumulation of fluid in the abdomen or chest (ascites, pleural effusion)
  • Dry (non-effusive) form: Granulomatous lesions in organs (kidneys, liver, eyes, brain)

2. Early Signs of FIP

  • Persistent fever – unresponsive to antibiotics
  • Weight loss and muscle wasting
  • Lethargy and decreased appetite
  • Distended abdomen (wet form) – appears "pot-bellied"
  • Jaundice (yellow gums, eyes)
  • Eye changes (uveitis, cloudy eyes)
  • Neurological signs (seizures, incoordination)
⚠️ If your cat shows these signs, especially fever and weight loss, see your vet immediately. Early diagnosis improves treatment outcomes.

3. How Is FIP Diagnosed?

FIP is notoriously difficult to diagnose. There is no single test that confirms it definitively. Your vet will use a combination of:

  • Bloodwork: High globulin, low albumin, elevated white blood cells
  • Rivalta test: On abdominal fluid (for wet form)
  • PCR testing: Detects feline coronavirus (but not the FIP-causing mutation)
  • Immunohistochemistry: Biopsy of affected tissue (gold standard)
  • Imaging (X-rays, ultrasound): To check for fluid or organ damage

4. Treatment Breakthroughs: New Antiviral Therapies

Until recently, FIP was considered almost universally fatal. Today, antiviral drugs have changed the outlook dramatically.

  • GS-441524: An antiviral drug that inhibits viral replication. Has shown cure rates of 80-90% in clinical studies. Available through veterinary specialists and university hospitals.
  • Remdesivir: A prodrug of GS-441524, used for severe cases. Typically given as IV injections.
  • Treatment duration: Typically 12-16 weeks of daily injections or oral medication.
  • Monitoring: Regular bloodwork and veterinary check-ups during and after treatment.

5. Supportive Care

  • Nutritional support: High-quality, easily digestible food
  • Fluid therapy: IV fluids to maintain hydration
  • Anti-inflammatory drugs: To reduce inflammation
  • Pain management: To keep your cat comfortable

7. What to Avoid

  • Waiting too long – early treatment is key
  • Ignoring signs – FIP progresses rapidly
  • Using unproven treatments – many are ineffective
  • Not seeking specialist care – veterinary internists or FIP specialists have the most experience

8. Your Action Plan

  1. If your cat shows signs of FIP (fever, weight loss, lethargy), see your vet immediately
  2. Ask about FIP testing (bloodwork, fluid analysis, PCR)
  3. If diagnosed, discuss antiviral therapy options (GS-441524, remdesivir)
  4. Provide supportive care (nutrition, fluids, pain management)
  5. Work with a veterinary specialist for the best outcome
